# Content design pattern — first slice

## Purpose

Make a research topic useful as an experience, not just an article. Content should connect concept → relationships → optional demonstration → transparent result → evidence → next learning step.

## Progressive content levels

Every concept can add depth without making the first screen dense:

1. **Simple:** one or two clear sentences with everyday language.
2. **Practical:** an example of where the concept appears in learning or daily life.
3. **Academic:** standard terminology and a concise model.
4. **Technical:** mechanisms, competing explanations, and methods where justified.
5. **Research:** original/review sources, population, outcomes, debate, and limitations.

The user controls depth. Do not force a child/beginner to read a paper before trying the concept, and do not simplify technical uncertainty into certainty.

## Concept page sections

- **What it is:** concise, source-consistent definition.
- **Why it matters:** a qualified explanation, without claiming that one factor determines the whole person.
- **Related concepts:** labeled and typed relationships, with a text alternative.
- **Try it:** clearly framed educational demonstration, never disguised diagnosis.
- **Your result:** observable task metrics plus limitations; no unsupported interpretation.
- **Evidence:** exact claim, linked source, population/outcome, evidence rationale, limitations, and review date.
- **Go deeper:** optional academic/research material.
- **Next:** one or two relevant paths, not a crowded recommendation grid.

## Relationship language

Use explicit edge types such as `part_of`, `related_to`, `measured_by`, `practiced_by`, `evidence_for`, or `evidence_against`. Display the type in words. Do not encode causal claims in an arrow unless the linked evidence supports causation; prefer “related to” when the direction is uncertain or associative.

## Language and voice

- Calm, curious, clear, and non-judgmental.
- Short headings and paragraphs; explain each technical term when first used.
- Avoid “brain hack,” “rewire,” “unlock,” “boost IQ,” “brain age,” and motivational pressure.
- Separate known evidence from hypotheses, analogy, and product suggestion.
- No fake neuroscience visualization; label simplified diagrams as conceptual models.
- Keep source URLs/title/author/date distinct from the product's interpretation of the source.

## First memory lesson: proposed outline

1. A simple definition of memory and the narrow idea of working memory.
2. A small relationship map (memory, working memory, attention, encoding, retrieval, learning).
3. A self-paced recall demonstration.
4. A plain result such as “You recalled 4 of 6 words in this round.”
5. A visible boundary: this is a single task, not a validated assessment or measure of general memory/IQ/health.
6. A source card on the limited-capacity model and a separate source card on retrieval practice; distinguish the general research result from what this mini-demo itself demonstrates.

## Content review checklist

- [ ] Every factual claim has an evidence-ledger ID and verified source.
- [ ] Wording does not exceed source population, outcome, design, or follow-up.
- [ ] Uncertainty, limitations, and transfer level appear near the claim.
- [ ] Relationship types are not mistaken for causation.
- [ ] Beginners can understand the first layer; advanced depth is optional.
- [ ] The result is a task-level observation, not a personal diagnosis/profile.
- [ ] Content renders readably without graph visuals, audio, or AI.
